## Why there is code in a paper-review assignment

The survey catalogues which bandit formulation each application paper used, but
it never compares the policies on common ground, and it runs no experiment of
its own. The absence of a shared benchmark is one of the open problems the
authors themselves list. We built the smallest honest version of that benchmark
so that our discussion of the algorithms rests on numbers we produced rather
than on claims we repeated.

## Files

| File | What it does |
|---|---|
| `bandit_experiment.py` | 10-arm Bernoulli bandit. Compares a random baseline, epsilon-greedy, UCB1 and Thompson Sampling over 1000 steps and 200 runs. Prints the results table and writes both plots. |
| `ucb1_trace.py` | Stops UCB1 at step 50 and prints every term of the index rule for all ten arms, so the next selection can be verified on a calculator. |
| `tiebreak_study.py` | Follow-up study. Measures how often UCB1 index ties occur, and whether the tie-breaking rule changes final regret. |
| `regret_curves.png` | Cumulative regret, mean of 200 runs. Generated by the first script. |
| `final_regret.png` | Final regret with standard deviation across runs. Generated by the first script. |

## How to run

Requires Python 3 with `numpy` and `matplotlib`.

```bash
python3 bandit_experiment.py
python3 ucb1_trace.py
```

The seed is fixed at 124, our group number. Both scripts are deterministic, so
every number in our presentation reproduces exactly.

## Results (seed 124, 1000 steps, 200 runs)

| policy | cumulative regret | std | % optimal arm, last 100 steps |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Random baseline | 162.10 | 3.85 | 9.7 |
| epsilon-greedy (0.1) | **52.09** | 28.24 | 39.7 |
| UCB1 | 99.93 | 7.10 | 25.9 |
| Thompson Sampling | 55.10 | 13.74 | **48.8** |

Three observations:

1. UCB1 finishes worse than epsilon-greedy, which is not the ordering a textbook
   implies. The two best arms in our draw are 0.5846 and 0.5694, a gap of
   0.0151. UCB1's exploration bonus keeps both alive for a long time, and its
   regret guarantee is asymptotic; 1000 steps is nowhere near asymptotic at that
   gap.
2. Epsilon-greedy wins on mean regret but has nearly four times UCB1's standard
   deviation. It sometimes commits early to the second-best arm and never
   recovers. Mean regret alone hides that failure mode.
3. Thompson Sampling is marginally worse on the mean but halves the variance and
   finds the optimal arm most often.

The second point supports our main criticism of the survey: it ranks nothing, and
when you do rank these policies the answer turns out to depend on the arm gap.

## What is ours and what is a library

Ours: the environment, all four policy functions, the incremental mean update,
the regret accounting, the run loop, and both analysis scripts.

Library: `numpy` for the random number generator and array arithmetic,
`matplotlib` for the two plots. No bandit or reinforcement learning library is
used, and none of the policies are imported.

## Follow-up: the tie-breaking question

`ucb1_trace.py` showed that at step 51 four arms tie at exactly 1.644818. They
share a pull count (6) and an empirical mean (0.5). This is structural rather
than coincidental: Bernoulli rewards mean that after n pulls an empirical mean
can take only n + 1 distinct values, so collisions are common while n is small.

`numpy.argmax` resolves a tie by returning the lowest index, which is an
implementation choice rather than part of the UCB1 rule. We expected this to
matter, so we measured it.

| question | answer |
|---|---|
| How often do ties occur? | 8.6% of all steps. 45.6% of the first 100 steps, falling to 2.8% after step 500. Mean 2.56 arms tied; largest observed tie was 9. |
| Does the rule change final regret? | No. Uniform-random tie-breaking versus lowest-index shifts regret by 0.86 across 200 paired runs, 95% CI [-0.36, 2.08], t = 1.38. Run-to-run std is 7.19. |
| Does arm ordering interact with the deterministic rule? | Sorting the arms best-first versus worst-first moves regret by 0.65, also inside the noise. |

So our initial suspicion was wrong, and we report it as a negative result. The
mechanism is straightforward in hindsight: tied arms have equal index, so
whichever is not chosen remains tied and is selected within a few steps. The
rule changes the order of pulls, not which arms get pulled.

The frequency finding still stands on its own. Ties are common enough in the
early phase that any paper reporting UCB1 results on a Bernoulli bandit is
making a tie-breaking choice, and none of the papers in the survey state what
theirs is.
